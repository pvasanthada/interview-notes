# Mentoring & Growing Others

**Label:** `Interview Preparation`

## Why This Matters at Principal Level
A principal architect's impact is bounded by how much of their judgment can be replicated by others. Mentoring — both formal and through everyday decision-making — is one of the highest-leverage activities at this level, and interviewers specifically probe for it because it distinguishes a strong senior IC from someone ready for organization-wide technical influence.

## Core Practices
- **Teach the reasoning, not just the answer.** When reviewing a design or troubleshooting a problem with someone, narrate the *why* behind your suggestions, not just the fix — this is the difference between solving today's problem and growing the person's future judgment.
- **Create stretch opportunities deliberately.** Delegate a genuinely ambiguous, meaningful problem (not just busywork) to someone ready to grow, with enough support that failure is a learning opportunity, not a disaster.
- **Give feedback that's specific and actionable**, close to the moment it's earned, not saved up for a formal review cycle.
- **Model the behavior you want to see** — asking clarifying questions, admitting uncertainty, stating trade-offs explicitly — since this is often absorbed more effectively by example than by instruction.
- **Sponsor, not just support.** Actively advocate for high-potential engineers' visibility and opportunities in rooms they aren't in, not just help them when asked.

## Common Failure Patterns
- Giving answers instead of teaching reasoning, creating dependency rather than growth.
- Only mentoring people who remind you of yourself, narrowing the diversity of technical perspective the organization develops.
- Reserving feedback for formal review cycles instead of giving it close to the moment.

## Principal-Level Interview Questions
- Describe a time you helped grow someone's technical judgment, not just their specific skill.
- How do you decide when to let someone struggle productively versus stepping in?

## Scenario Question
> A promising mid-level engineer on your team consistently produces technically correct designs that miss important trade-offs (security, cost, operational burden) that a more senior engineer would catch.

## Strong Answer (Outline)
1. Rather than simply correcting each design after the fact, make the missing dimensions explicit and repeatable — introduce them to the [Technical Question Framework](../docs/answering-frameworks.md#1-technical-question-framework) as a structured checklist they can apply themselves before bringing a design for review.
2. Pair on a real, current design together, narrating my own reasoning through each trade-off dimension out loud, rather than just critiquing their finished work.
3. Give them a genuinely ambiguous problem with real stakes (not a toy exercise) and check in at defined milestones rather than reviewing only the final output — this builds the muscle of catching these dimensions themselves under real conditions.
4. Recognize and reinforce it explicitly and promptly when I see them catch a trade-off they'd previously missed, so the growth is visible to them too.

## Follow-Up Questions
- How do you balance giving someone room to grow against the risk of a real production issue if they miss something important?
- How do you mentor someone whose working style is very different from your own?
